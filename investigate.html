Case studyCisco Umbrella, 2020 to 2022, my first PM role

Integrated where it helped analysts, standalone where customers needed it

Investigate was a paid threat-intelligence product, sold through its own app and an API. My director suggested folding it fully into Cisco Umbrella, Cisco's cloud security product. I brought it into Umbrella where it helped analysts, kept the standalone app for the customers who needed it, and cleaned up the API.

The two-minute version

The product
Investigate gave security teams threat intelligence on domains, IPs and ASNs, through a console and an API. Customers included Fortune 500 companies, universities and government organizations.
The problem
Investigate was a separate product from Umbrella, with its own authentication. Investigation workflows were fragmented, and the API's access model didn't match the rest of Umbrella.
The key call
My director suggested folding Investigate fully into Umbrella and rebranding it as a data feed. Research and marketing showed that would hurt customers who had Investigate without Umbrella, advanced researchers, and possibly the sales pipeline. I kept the standalone product, integrated where it helped analysts, and presented that as a strategy. The director agreed.
What I did
Brought Investigate into the Umbrella console as a micro-app, cleaned up the API without breaking changes, and decided to unify its authentication with Umbrella's, which the platform team built.
What changed
API revenue rose 10%, a gain I attribute to the API cleanup, and API-related support tickets fell 35%. Admins who need a quick verdict scrolled less and used Investigate more, and researchers kept the full standalone experience. Usage went up, Investigate became part of regular investigations, and access and login tickets fell after the auth merge.
What I'd change
Set explicit success criteria for each phase before starting it, so the call to move on is a decision, not a default.

Useful intelligence, outside the workflow

Investigate's users were technical: security researchers, incident responders and threat hunters, plus teams that pulled its data into their own tools through the API.

It sat apart from Umbrella, with its own authentication, so investigation workflows were fragmented across the two. The API had its own problems: access-management issues, throttling and latency. API-related questions were a source of support tickets that a cleaner API could avoid.

One option was to merge Investigate into Umbrella completely. Part of this case is why I didn't.

I started with customers, and the people who talk to them every day

I spoke with customers directly, from enterprises to universities, across different verticals, and worked with our research team on more customer calls and recordings. I also interviewed about 40 customer success managers, account managers and support staff, and walked through the customer lifecycle with them: onboarding, day-to-day use, and incidents. Three kinds of user came out of it.

Quick verdict

Who
IT and network admins who see a domain in their logs
What they need
Block it or isolate it, and what blocking it would break

Deep dive

Who
Security researchers, SOC analysts, and incident responders writing postmortems
What they need
Interconnected DNS, firewall, file hash and IP data, explored as a graph

Automation

Who
Mostly enterprise teams building pipelines through the API
What they need
Threat intelligence fed into their SIEM and SOAR tools, and into automated block and allow rules

One finding that shaped the design

Analysts didn't just need a verdict on a domain. They needed to know what blocking it would break. For example, a Twitter content domain might show up in a customer's DNS logs. Blocking it could break things the marketing team relied on. So I moved risk indicators to the top of the page and added hover insights, working with UX, security researchers and data scientists.

The main decision

I kept Investigate standalone and integrated where it helped analysts

My skip-level manager, the director of product, suggested I consider a full integration: Investigate as a micro-app inside Umbrella with no standalone experience, rebranded by marketing and sales as a data feed.

Input from research and marketing showed who would lose out. Some customers had Investigate without Umbrella, and advanced researchers depended on the full standalone experience. It could also have disrupted the sales pipeline, since sales used Investigate to show Umbrella's efficacy.

So I kept the standalone web experience with fewer integration points. I presented it to the director as a strategy built on the three kinds of user, with a direction we called threat intelligence everywhere. The director agreed. I wrote that strategy a few months into my first PM role.

Two more calls, and one constraint that wasn't mine

Decision

One access model

The call
Remove Investigate's separate authentication and merge it with Umbrella's, so customers had one access model
Why
Customer conversations, UX research and CSMs all pointed the same way
My part
The product decision, and the API access model in the console: UX integration, user permissions and API token usage. The platform team built the merge.
Result
Fewer access and login tickets, and fewer complaints from customers and internal teams

Decision

A late launch over throwaway work

What happened
The platform team's work to host Investigate as a micro-app took longer than they had estimated
The call
Accept a later date rather than build a workaround we'd throw away, and use the time to upgrade existing features so customers still got improvements, such as an interactive DNS traffic chart with a selectable time range
Result
The micro-app launched about a month late

Constraint

OAuth and API usage tracking waited

What happened
Umbrella was building a common API platform for the whole product, so OAuth and consumption tracking were left to it
Whose call
Org priorities, not mine. I agreed with the reasoning: we shouldn't build it twice.
Meanwhile
Customers tracked their usage themselves or asked their account reps

Four phases, starting with early validation

The console integration went first, to validate early and get feedback from customers and the field. About eight months end to end, with six engineers, an engineering manager and shared UX support.

  1. 1. Console integration About 3 to 4 months
    Investigate rebuilt as a micro-app on the Umbrella platform, with the platform and UX teams, for easier integration, a consistent UX and no duplicate work.
  2. 2. API cleanup
    Prioritized by revenue and by what mattered to customers, with no breaking changes. I owned the API contract and endpoints, and versioning and deprecation decisions.
  3. 3. More data points
    New data to line up with Umbrella's product features, such as whether a domain belongs to a SaaS service.
  4. 4. Data platform Did not launch
    The plan was for Cisco Talos to take over the feeds Investigate used, and for Investigate to read from Talos. SecureX had already been consuming Investigate's feeds directly.

What changed for each kind of user, and how I know

Only the API results have numbers I measured and can stand behind. The rest are signals I saw in product analytics and support tickets, without figures I can check today.

Quick verdict

Risk indicators at the top of the page, hover insights, and context such as whether a domain belongs to a SaaS service
What changed
Users scrolled less and used Investigate more
How I know
Product analytics, before and after

Deep dive

What shipped
The standalone app stayed, with an interactive DNS traffic chart and more data points
What changed

How I know
No separate measure for this group

Automation

What shipped
A cleaned-up API with no breaking changes, prioritized by revenue and by what mattered to customers
What changed
API revenue rose 10%, and API-related support tickets fell 35%
How I know
I measured both before and after with our analytics tools, and I attribute the revenue gain to the API cleanup

Across all three, Investigate became part of the workflow. After the console integration, click heat maps showed customers opening Investigate from the Umbrella console and then returning to their investigation. That pattern meant it was becoming part of their workflow, not a one-time spike. After the auth merge, access and login tickets fell, and so did complaints from customers and internal teams.

What I don't claim

I don't put numbers on usage, scrolling or login tickets. I left Cisco in 2022, Cisco retired Investigate around October 2026, and I can't check the original figures.

What I'd do differently

Agree on a bar for each phase before starting it

When we finished the console integration and moved to the API, I couldn't tell leadership, or honestly myself, whether the first phase had worked. We had signals like the heat maps, but not a bar we'd agreed on up front.

What I do now: make the go or no-go call explicit, with impact and cost on the table. At AWS, when fully automating customer onboarding for the Migration Acceleration Program turned out more complex than expected, I made it an explicit decision and chose a smaller version that still gave customers real value.
