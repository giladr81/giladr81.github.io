This is my own work. I'm not affiliated with Outside, Gaia GPS or onX in any way, and nobody there asked for or reviewed it. It's built from public reviews, forums and product pages, with no access to company data. Treat it as a starting point: the findings need further research and user discovery to confirm them, and then the actual product delivery work.

The two apps

Gaia GPS is a navigation app for hiking, camping, overlanding and off-roading, built around offline maps for places with no cell service. It's part of the Outside network of outdoor brands, free to use, with a paid Premium subscription (Apple App Store listing).

onX Offroad is built for off-roaders: trail maps for 4x4s, side-by-sides, ATVs and dirt bikes, plus offline maps, route planning and land ownership. It's also free to use, with paid Premium and Elite plans (onX's product page).

Both are used far from cell service, so an app that fails there fails at the worst time.

The two-minute version

The question
Long-time users make up a large share of Gaia's unhappy reviewers and almost none of onX's. What are they unhappy about: the paywall, failures, or something reviews barely measure, such as trail discovery?
What the reviews show
Two complaints dominate. Paywall complaints are equally common on both stores, mostly about route saving moving behind the paywall. Failures during a trip concentrate on the Apple App Store, where Gaia's unhappy reviewers report them as often as onX's. Among long-time critics alone, there are too few reviews to tell whether that gap between the stores is real.
My hypothesis
Two readings fit the reviews. The paywall loses long-time free users, or failures lose subscribers at renewal. Price is named at least as often as failures among long-time critics on both stores, so I wouldn't assume it is failures. Trail discovery may matter too: onX recommends trails, and I haven't found anything like that in Gaia.
What I'd do first
If I owned the product, I would first ask lapsed long-time users why they left, because the reviews are split between price and failures. In parallel I would add CarPlay and Android Auto checks to every release, since users reported a blank CarPlay map after and that is worth fixing either way. Then I would test a small route-saving allowance against a holdout.
What would change the plan
If lapsed users name price, the paywall or removed map layers first, the allowance test moves ahead of reliability work. If they name failures, the release checks move up. If neither, trail discovery is the next question.
What this can't tell you
Reviews show who complains, not who cancels. Gaia is also the older app, so it has more long-time users to begin with. Gaia and onX 1 and 2-star Apple App Store reviews show where to look, not how big the problem is. Only product data can size it: cancellations, offline launches and crashes.

I compared Apple App Store reviews of Gaia GPS with of onX Offroad from to , the period both review feeds fully cover (method), and sorted each complaint into price, route planning or the app failing during a trip, then repeated the comparison on the Google Play Store (Android).

Approach

How I approached it

I started as a user weighing a switch to onX, and turned that into a question: why are long-time users unhappy, and is it price or failures?

  1. Collect what users wrote

    Public reviews of Gaia GPS and onX Offroad from the Apple App Store (iPhone, US and Canada) and the Google Play Store (Android), compared over the period both review feeds fully cover.

  2. Sort the complaints

    Each 1 and 2-star review sorted into price, route planning or the app failing during a trip, and flagged as a long-time user from phrases like "for years" and "used to love". Hand checks on random samples measure how often the sorting is right.

  3. Compare, then try to break the finding

    Gaia against onX on the Apple App Store, then the same comparison on the Google Play Store, to separate the apps from the store. A result that doesn't hold on both stores is reported as such.

  4. Check other channels

    Reddit, the Gaia GPS forum and BC off-road Facebook groups, to see whether the same problems show up outside the app stores.

  5. Turn the findings into checks

    The cheapest checks on product data that would separate price from failures.

Who leaves, and which job fails

The complaints map to four jobs: keeping the routes I've built (planning and saving), navigating from the vehicle (CarPlay and Android Auto), finding the way with no signal (offline maps and login), and keeping a record (tracks and sync). The BC groups add a fact that changes the risk: members run two to four apps plus paper maps. So the threat isn't a dramatic switch. Gaia gets demoted to the backup app, and backups don't get renewed. Lock-in slows even that: on r/GaiaGPS, long-time users say years of saved tracks and waypoints keep them from leaving, so an unhappy user can stay subscribed while their trips move to another app. That's a hypothesis to check against product activity data.

Why onX Offroad. It's the app Gaia's critics name most as where they're going ( reviews), and the one I'm weighing. It isn't Gaia's whole market, and I can't size the off-road part from reviews: only Gaia reviews mention off-roading. The few unhappy ones say what the rest of this page says: crashes, price, and moving to onX. For hikers and hunters the comparison would be CalTopo or onX Backcountry, which I haven't run.

      If failures are the problem

      What I'd measure if failures drive cancellations

      If the checks below show that failures drive cancellations, this is what I would track, the way a team tracks uptime. An off-road navigation app makes one promise: the map works with no signal. Each target says how I'd set it.

      Opens with no signal

      What it measures
      Launches with no network that show a usable map and saved items without a login prompt.
      How to set the target
      Four weeks of offline launches by app version. Target the best recent version's rate.
      Starting guess
      Hypothesis99.9% or better, with a usable map within 5 seconds.

      Crash-free trips

      What it measures
      Crash-free sessions that start with no network or on CarPlay or Android Auto, tracked apart from use in town.
      How to set the target
      Close the gap to the in-town rate. Hold any release that blanks the CarPlay map in testing.

      Nothing lost in sync

      What it measures
      Tracks, waypoints and routes that reach the account intact within 24 hours of signal returning.
      How to set the target
      Zero lost items. Until then, unsynced items say "not synced yet".

      Order of work

      Order of work, if I owned this

      Ranked by what separates price from failures first, then by how many long-time critics each problem reaches (from reviews) and how badly it breaks a trip (my judgment: a lockout ends the trip, a crash costs minutes, a paywalled feature costs goodwill). Whether any of it pays back depends on how many cancellations follow a failure, which only product data can show.

      1. Ask lapsed long-time users why they left

        Reviews name price and failures about equally, so the first move is to ask. Ten short calls or a cancellation survey, including whether trail discovery or removed map layers played a part.

        How we'd know it worked: most lapsed users name one reason first. That reason sets what comes next.

      2. Make the in-vehicle map a release gate

        The blank CarPlay map reported from , after Gaia , shows how a regression reaches users, and no release note since mentions CarPlay. Test CarPlay and Android Auto before every release and track crash-free trips apart from in-town use, so the next regression shows up within one release.

        Reaches long-time critics.

        How we'd know it worked: CarPlay map failures at zero within two releases, and the field crash-free rate matching in-town use.

      3. Test a limited free route-saving allowance

        Long-time users built routes for years, then found saving them paywalled. Offer a random group of lapsed long-time free users a small free allowance, such as a few saved routes, against a holdout.

        Reaches long-time critics.

        How we'd know it worked: reactivation goes up with no drop in paid conversion. If conversion drops, the paywall stays and the upgrade message changes instead.

      4. Make the offline-login fix a rule with a metric

        Android fixed account re-authentication, and iOS loads downloaded maps more reliably when signal is weak. What's missing is a metric that proves it holds. It reaches fewer critics, but it does the most harm.

      What I wouldn't do yet

      • Cut the price before the allowance test. A lower price gives up revenue from subscribers who aren't complaining, and the test shows whether packaging is the issue.
      • Add new map layers before reliability holds. New content on an unreliable app makes each failure cost more.

      Checks

      Checks that separate price from failures

      Reviews show where to look, not what to do. Cheapest checks first, and each has a result that would change the order of work. Split every test by platform: reviews suggest failures matter more to Apple App Store users, and only product data can confirm it.

      Gaia's own recent releases point the same way. iOS () says downloaded maps load more reliably when cell service is spotty, and Android () fixed account re-authentication. The team has started on offline and login. So the first checks should run on these versions, or they'll measure problems that may already be fixed.

      Limits of this evidence, and how to close each one

      • Reviewers skew unhappy, so reviews compare problems, not overall satisfaction. Cancellation analysis would show it.
      • The head-to-head uses Apple App Store reviews, and Gaia has only in the period. Counted per 100 reviews, the failure gap looks like (range to ), but that count is inflated by how many happy users each app prompts to review, so the case uses shares of unhappy reviews. On the Google Play Store the failure gap is small. Repeating the comparison each quarter on both stores would show whether it holds.
      • Types come from phrase matching. In hand checks, of sampled reviews per type were correctly placed, and of for long-time users. Support-ticket categories would test it.
      • Gaia is the older app, so it has more users who could have used it for years. Part of the long-time gap may come from that, not from how each app treats its users. Comparing critics who started at the same time, from subscription start dates, would show it.
      • onX Offroad is a vehicle app. Only Gaia reviews mention off-roading, so for Gaia's hikers and hunters the comparison is untested. Splitting app data by activity, and repeating this against CalTopo or onX Backcountry, would show it.
